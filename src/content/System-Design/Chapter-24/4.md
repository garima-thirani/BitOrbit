# What Interviewers Actually Evaluate

Many candidates believe interviewers are looking for the "correct architecture."

They are not.

Instead, they evaluate whether you think like an engineer.

A typical evaluation looks like this:

| Area | What Interviewers Look For |
|-------|----------------------------|
| Requirement Gathering | Do you ask the right questions? |
| Capacity Estimation | Can you estimate scale? |
| Architecture | Can you design a scalable solution? |
| Technology Choices | Can you justify your decisions? |
| Trade-offs | Do you understand compromises? |
| Communication | Can you explain clearly? |
| Problem Solving | Can you adapt when requirements change? |

Notice that **communication is just as important as technical knowledge.**

# The First Five Minutes

The beginning of the interview is critical.

Never start drawing immediately.

Instead:

1. Clarify the problem.
2. Gather requirements.
3. Define assumptions.
4. Estimate scale.
5. Explain your approach.

Interviewers immediately notice structured thinking.

---

# Example

Suppose the interviewer says

> Design Instagram.

Don't begin drawing servers.

Instead ask:

- How many users?
- Global deployment?
- Photos only or videos?
- Stories?
- Messaging?
- Search?
- Expected latency?
- Availability requirements?

Five minutes of clarification often saves twenty minutes of redesign.

# Common Follow-Up Questions

Interviewers almost always ask follow-up questions.

For example:

> How would you scale it?

> What happens if Redis fails?

> What if one database shard becomes too large?

> How would you handle global users?

> What if traffic increases by 100×?

> How would you improve latency?

> What if one service crashes?

Prepare for these questions while designing.

# Common FAANG Mistakes

Many candidates fail because of avoidable mistakes.

## Mistake 1

Jumping into architecture without understanding requirements.

---

## Mistake 2

Ignoring capacity estimation.

---

## Mistake 3

Choosing technologies because they are popular.

Instead explain

**why**

they solve the problem.

---

## Mistake 4

Ignoring trade-offs.

Every decision has consequences.

Discuss them.

---

## Mistake 5

Designing everything as microservices.

Sometimes a monolith is the better solution.

---

## Mistake 6

Adding unnecessary complexity.

A simple solution that scales reasonably is usually better than an overly complicated design.

---

## Mistake 7

Ignoring failures.

Every production system eventually fails.

Explain

- retries
- replication
- failover
- disaster recovery

---

## Mistake 8

Forgetting observability.

Production systems need

- metrics
- logs
- traces
- alerting

---

## Mistake 9

Ignoring security.

Always consider

- authentication
- authorization
- rate limiting
- encryption
- secrets management

---

## Mistake 10

Poor communication.

A great architecture explained poorly often performs worse than a good architecture explained clearly.

# Final Advice

Remember:

System Design interviews are not exams.

They are engineering discussions.

The interviewer is not expecting perfection.

They want to understand **how you think**.

Communicate clearly.

State assumptions.

Explain trade-offs.

Design incrementally.

And most importantly,

treat the interview as a collaborative design session rather than a test.

---

# Final Key Takeaways

- Requirements always drive architecture.
- Capacity estimation should precede technology selection.
- Choose technologies based on problems they solve, not popularity.
- Explain trade-offs for every major architectural decision.
- Consider scalability, reliability, security, observability, and deployment in every design.
- Communicate your thought process continuously throughout the interview.
- Use structured decision frameworks instead of memorizing architectures.
- Practice complete end-to-end case studies regularly.
- Review comparison matrices and decision trees before interviews.
- Great System Design interviews are won through structured thinking, engineering judgment, and clear communication—not by memorizing diagrams.
