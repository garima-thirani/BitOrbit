# Introduction

In the previous part, we learned how search engines preprocess text before building an Inverted Index.

When a user searches,

```text
distributed systems
```

the search engine can now retrieve all documents containing the terms.

But another question immediately arises.

> **If thousands or even millions of documents match the query, which one should appear first?**

Simply finding matching documents is not enough.

A good search engine must determine:

- Which document is the most relevant?
- Which documents should appear at the top?
- Which documents should be ignored?

This process is known as **Ranking**.

Ranking is one of the most important aspects of any search engine because users rarely go beyond the first page of results.

---

# The Search Process

A search engine performs two major tasks.

1. **Retrieval**
   - Find all candidate documents.

2. **Ranking**
   - Order those documents from most relevant to least relevant.

```text
User Query
      │
      ▼
Retrieve Candidate Documents
      │
      ▼
Compute Relevance Scores
      │
      ▼
Rank Documents
      │
      ▼
Top Search Results
```

Without ranking,

search results would simply be returned in arbitrary order.

---

# Boolean Search

The simplest ranking model is **Boolean Search**.

Documents either satisfy the query or they do not.

There is no concept of "more relevant."

---

## AND Operator

Suppose we have three documents.

```text
D1

Distributed Systems using Kafka
```

```text
D2

Distributed Systems Handbook
```

```text
D3

Kafka Streams Guide
```

Query

```text
Distributed AND Kafka
```

Results

```text
D1
```

Only documents containing **both** terms are returned.

---

## OR Operator

Query

```text
Distributed OR Kafka
```

Results

```text
D1

D2

D3
```

Any matching document is returned.

---

## NOT Operator

Query

```text
Kafka NOT Streams
```

Result

```text
D1
```

---

## Limitations

Boolean Search has one major drawback.

Suppose two documents satisfy the query.

```text
D1

Kafka Kafka Kafka Kafka
```

```text
D2

Kafka
```

Boolean Search considers both equally relevant.

Clearly,

D1 discusses Kafka much more extensively.

We need a better ranking mechanism.

---

# Term Frequency (TF)

One simple observation is:

> The more often a word appears inside a document,
> the more relevant the document is likely to be.

This idea is called **Term Frequency (TF).**

---

## Example

Query

```text
Kafka
```

Documents

```text
D1

Kafka Streams with Kafka Producers
```

Occurrences

```text
Kafka

↓

2 times
```

---

```text
D2

Kafka Tutorial
```

Occurrences

```text
Kafka

↓

1 time
```

TF suggests

```text
D1

>

D2
```

because Kafka appears more frequently.

---

## Formula

A simplified TF formula is

```text
TF = Number of occurrences of the term
     -----------------------------------
      Total words in the document
```

Higher TF generally indicates stronger relevance.

However,

TF alone introduces another problem.

---

# The Problem with Term Frequency

Suppose the query is

```text
the
```

Every document contains

```text
the
```

hundreds of times.

Does that mean every document is highly relevant?

Of course not.

Common words should contribute very little to ranking.

This leads us to **Inverse Document Frequency.**

---

# Inverse Document Frequency (IDF)

IDF measures

> **How rare or unique a word is across all documents.**

Rare words provide more information than common words.

---

## Example

Imagine a search index containing one million documents.

Word

```text
Kafka
```

appears in

```text
3,000
```

documents.

Word

```text
the
```

appears in

```text
999,900
```

documents.

Clearly,

Kafka is much more informative than "the."

Therefore,

Kafka receives a much higher IDF score.

---

## Intuition

Rare words

↓

High IDF

Common words

↓

Low IDF

---

## Simplified Formula

```text
IDF = log(
      Total Documents
      -----------------
      Documents containing the term
)
```

The fewer documents containing the term,

the larger the IDF value.

---

# TF-IDF

TF and IDF complement each other.

TF measures

> Importance within a document.

IDF measures

> Importance across the entire collection.

Together,

they form **TF-IDF**.

---

## Formula

```text
TF-IDF

=

TF

×

IDF
```

---

## Example

Suppose the query is

```text
Kafka
```

Document A

- Kafka appears 12 times

Document B

- Kafka appears once

If Kafka itself is relatively uncommon,

Document A receives a significantly higher score.

TF-IDF rewards

- frequent
- informative

terms simultaneously.

---

## Why TF-IDF Was Revolutionary

Before TF-IDF,

search engines simply matched keywords.

TF-IDF introduced

**statistical relevance.**

Instead of asking

> Does the document contain the word?

Search engines asked

> How important is this word in this document compared to every other document?

This dramatically improved search quality.

---

# BM25

Modern search engines rarely use raw TF-IDF anymore.

Instead,

they use an improved ranking algorithm called **BM25**.

BM25 is currently the default ranking algorithm in:

- Elasticsearch
- OpenSearch
- Apache Lucene

---

## Why TF-IDF Isn't Enough

Consider two documents.

Document A

```text
100 words
```

Document B

```text
10,000 words
```

Both contain

```text
Kafka
```

20 times.

TF-IDF assigns similar importance.

But intuitively,

20 occurrences inside a 100-word document are much more significant.

BM25 adjusts for document length.

---

## Advantages of BM25

BM25 considers

- Term Frequency
- Document Frequency
- Document Length
- Saturation Effects

This produces much more accurate rankings.

---

## Why Search Engines Prefer BM25

BM25

- avoids excessively rewarding repeated words
- handles long documents better
- improves ranking quality
- works well across billions of documents

For this reason,

BM25 has become the industry standard.

---

# Phrase Search

Sometimes users care about exact phrases rather than individual words.

Suppose the query is

```text
system design interview
```

Document A

```text
System Design Interview
```

Document B

```text
Interview about distributed system design
```

Both contain the same words.

However,

Document A preserves the exact phrase.

Phrase Search identifies this difference.

---

## How Phrase Search Works

Remember the Posting Lists we discussed earlier?

Each posting stores word positions.

Example

```text
System

↓

Position 1
```

```text
Design

↓

Position 2
```

```text
Interview

↓

Position 3
```

Since all words appear consecutively,

the search engine recognizes an exact phrase.

---

# Fuzzy Search

Users frequently make typing mistakes.

Examples

```text
Docker

↓

Docer
```

```text
Redis

↓

Rdis
```

```text
Kubernetes

↓

Kuberenetes
```

A traditional search engine would return

"No Results Found."

Fuzzy Search attempts to identify similar words.

---

## Edit Distance

The most common technique is **Levenshtein Distance**.

It measures

> the minimum number of edits required to transform one word into another.

Example

```text
redis

↓

redsi
```

Only one swap is required.

The distance is very small.

The search engine concludes

```text
Did you mean Redis?
```

---

## Where Fuzzy Search is Used

- Google Search
- Amazon Search
- GitHub Search
- IDE Search
- File Search
- Product Search

Almost every modern search engine supports fuzzy matching.

---

# Search Ranking Pipeline

Everything we have learned now comes together.

```text
                 User Query
                      │
                      ▼
             Query Processing
                      │
                      ▼
           Retrieve Candidate Documents
                      │
                      ▼
              Boolean Filtering
                      │
                      ▼
             Compute TF-IDF / BM25
                      │
                      ▼
             Phrase Matching
                      │
                      ▼
              Fuzzy Matching
                      │
                      ▼
         Compute Final Relevance Score
                      │
                      ▼
              Rank All Documents
                      │
                      ▼
              Return Top Results
```

Notice that ranking is not determined by a single algorithm.

Modern search engines combine multiple scoring techniques before producing the final ordered list.

---

# Real-World Example

Suppose a developer searches GitHub for

```text
distributed cache
```

The search engine

- retrieves repositories containing both terms,
- removes low-quality matches,
- computes BM25 scores,
- boosts exact phrase matches,
- considers repository popularity,
- and finally ranks the repositories before returning the results.

Similarly,

Amazon combines BM25-like ranking with

- popularity,
- purchase history,
- ratings,
- personalization,
- and business signals

to determine which products appear first.

Ranking is therefore a combination of **Information Retrieval algorithms** and **application-specific relevance signals**.

---

# Key Takeaways

- Retrieving documents and ranking documents are two separate stages of search.
- Boolean Search filters documents but does not measure relevance.
- Term Frequency (TF) measures how often a term appears within a document.
- Inverse Document Frequency (IDF) measures how informative a term is across the document collection.
- TF-IDF combines local importance with global rarity to compute relevance.
- BM25 improves TF-IDF by accounting for document length and term saturation, making it the industry-standard ranking algorithm.
- Phrase Search identifies exact sequences of words using positional information stored in Posting Lists.
- Fuzzy Search handles spelling mistakes using techniques such as Edit Distance.
- Modern search engines combine multiple ranking signals before returning the final ordered list of results.
- In the next part, we will study **Elasticsearch Architecture**, the distributed search engine that implements these Information Retrieval concepts at internet scale.
